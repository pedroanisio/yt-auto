# Attention, curiosity and stakes

Why a viewer keeps watching a factual film, what the evidence says moves attention, and how stakes are built without lying. Each rule is stated so a writer can apply it and a reviewer (or a rule in `yt script --review`) can check it, at the strength the sources support after verification, not the strength the folklore gives it.

Source kinds are marked inline: peer-reviewed, PhD thesis, platform documentation, practitioner, or secondary. Where a number comes from an abstract or a search snippet rather than a page that was opened, the rule says so. Rules whose only sources could not be reached are not rules; they sit under "Folklore and open questions" with their status.

Shared rules. This file is the home for the curiosity-gap evidence (rules 1–3), the misconception evidence (rule 7), the Zeigarnik objection (Contested) and the stakes rules. The 30-second Intro metric is defined in `judging-narrative.md` rule 1 and rule 27 here only applies it; words-per-minute thresholds live in `direction-and-narration.md` rule 25 and rule 28 here defers to them; the on-screen-text and redundancy rule is `direction-and-narration.md` rule 2, summarised in rule 23 here; the position of the misconception in the film is `structure-and-hooks.md` rule 15.

## Opening the gap

1. **Open the film, and each major section, on a specific information gap — a concrete question, an outcome the viewer can anticipate but not resolve, or an expectation the facts will break — and name what will be resolved rather than only teasing that something will.**
Loewenstein's information-gap account treats curiosity as deprivation felt when a gap in knowledge becomes salient, and lists four situational triggers: a posed question or puzzle, a sequence with an anticipated but unknown resolution, a violated expectation, and information someone else holds (George Loewenstein, "The Psychology of Curiosity: A Review and Reinterpretation", Psychological Bulletin 116(1), 1994, pp. 90–91, https://www.byrdseed.com/handouts/Psychology%20of%20Curiosity.pdf — peer-reviewed review; the list was re-checked against the text layer of that copy). The list is illustrative, not closed, and the triggers section is theory with one supporting experiment: about 70% of subjects chose to learn the rule that closed the larger gap. Verification dropped the original "never open with background" clause: the same paper reports people are more curious about items they already know something about, so the viewer needs a foothold before the gap can be felt (Celeste Kidd and Benjamin Hayden, "The Psychology and Neuroscience of Curiosity", Neuron 88(3), 2015, https://www.kiddlab.com/_files/ugd/0975fd_090ef66c1f824ba0a685aaffeff9bb59.pdf — peer-reviewed perspective). Give the minimum grounding that lets the viewer hold a partial expectation, then break it; do not lead with extended background or definitions.
Check: for the cold open and each section opener, the reviewer tags the trigger used (question / unresolved outcome / expectation violation / someone-knows) and whether the gap is named concretely. Fail if the first three sentences contain no trigger. A rule can look for an explicit question mark or a "you'd expect X, but Y" construction.

2. **Before a key reveal, state the audience's likely guess and then say whether it was right; committing to a guess and being told it is wrong is what makes a gap impossible to ignore.**
Loewenstein reports that guessing alone and feedback alone did not raise self-reported curiosity, but the combination did, and feedback right after each guess raised it more than delayed feedback (Loewenstein 1994, pp. 91–92, URL above; the underlying experiments are an unpublished 1992 working paper reported second-hand, with curiosity as the outcome, not memory). Kang and colleagues found that, among trivia answers people guessed wrong, higher curiosity at encoding predicted better recall one to two weeks later (d = 1.11 high vs low curiosity, n = 11–13), and memory-related regions were more active after wrong guesses (Min Jeong Kang et al., "The Wick in the Candle of Learning: Epistemic Curiosity Activates Reward Circuitry and Enhances Memory", Psychological Science 20(8), 2009, https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf — peer-reviewed). Verification weakened the rule: neither source compares a wrong-guess-then-reveal against a reveal with no guess, and neither tests the rhetorical stand-in a film must use ("most people think X — hold that thought"). Treat the stand-in as the best available proxy, not a tested device.
Check: for each major reveal, (a) the audience's likely intuition or a prediction prompt appears before it, and (b) the reveal explicitly scores that intuition. A rule can flag any sentence introducing a new key fact that is not preceded within N sentences by "you might think / most people assume / guess" phrasing.

3. **For low-stakes, trivia-shaped hooks, pitch the question at what the audience half-knows; where the information matters to them, total unknowns are fair game; in either case the answer must not become inferable before the reveal.**
Self-reported curiosity about trivia peaks near 50% confidence, and this inverted-U has replicated (Kang et al. 2009, URL above: r = .44 with P(1−P), n = 19; Rachit Dubey and Thomas Griffiths, "Reconciling novelty and complexity through a rational analysis of curiosity", Psychological Review 127(3), 2020, https://cocosci.dreamhosters.com/papers/curiosity.pdf — peer-reviewed, Exp. 1 n = 253). But Dubey and Griffiths' own experiment shows the curve "goes away" when the information's future usefulness is decoupled from familiarity: in that condition curiosity was highest for the least-known items (b = −0.27, p < .05). So either build a foothold first or make the stakes explicit, and in the latter case the unknown is the draw. The "do not let the answer leak" clause rests on Loewenstein's jigsaw and murder-mystery argument (1994, p. 90): theory, not data. Nothing here was tested on titles, thumbnails or narration; the infant "Goldilocks" data in Kidd and Hayden are analogy.
Check: at the ideas stage, each candidate question carries a line "what does a typical viewer already believe about this?"; reject questions where the honest answer is "nothing" unless the plan states why the answer matters to the viewer. In script review, a reader stops at the reveal and writes down their guess; if it is right, the loop leaked.

4. **Title, thumbnail and hook lines name the concrete subject and withhold exactly one thing — the mechanism or the outcome; fully vague "you won't believe" framing lowers clicks in most tests.**
A registered-report analysis of 8,977 Upworthy headline A/B tests (35,910 headline observations) found click-through maximised at middling concreteness: in the half of tests where the competing headlines were already concrete, more concreteness lowered clicks; in the 8.7% of tests that were vague, it raised them (interaction −0.058, p < 0.001) ("When curiosity gaps backfire: effects of headline concreteness on information selection decisions", 2024, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11704130/ — peer-reviewed registered report). The same paper's Table 1 relays a lab study (Scacco and Muddiman, N = 2,057) in which question headlines slightly reduced intention to read. This is headline data from one outlet's audience; transfer to video openings is an assumption, but it agrees with rule 1: a gap must be specific to be felt.
Check: the title and the first hook line each contain at least one specific named entity and withhold one causal link ("why X does Y", not "the truth about X"). Fail titles with no concrete noun, titles that already contain the answer, and a fixed list of empty forward-reference phrases.

## Paying the gap

5. **Put the payoff itself inside the window between the question and its answer; use that window for at most one or two secondary facts, not for a dense block the viewer must carry.**
Raising curiosity about a question strongly improved memory for the answer (70.6% vs 54.1% recall immediately; 45.9% vs 28.1% a day later), with midbrain and nucleus accumbens activity rising during the wait (Matthias Gruber, Bernard Gelman and Charan Ranganath, "States of curiosity modulate hippocampus-dependent learning via the dopaminergic circuit", Neuron 84(2), 2014, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/ — peer-reviewed). The spill-over to unrelated material shown during the wait was real but small: one neutral face per trial, recognised 42.4% vs 38.2% immediately (one-tailed p = 0.032) and, at one day, only among confidently recognised faces. Verification dropped the original "everything encoded in the window sticks": no verbal or semantic content was tested, and the authors call the incidental effect "relatively subtle". Kang et al.'s one-to-two-week finding (2009, URL above) concerns the answers, specifically surprising ones, not incidental material.
Check: map each open question to the fact that answers it (the promise/payoff ledger). Flag any must-remember fact delivered while no question is open, and flag any open-to-close interval carrying more than two secondary facts. What counts as secondary is taste only.

6. **Deliver at least what the hook promised, and frame the resolution as something gained; satisfaction that falls short of the curiosity raised is associated with worse memory.**
Marvin and Shohamy define an information prediction error as satisfaction on receipt minus curiosity in anticipation and found memory better for items with a positive error (odds ratio 1.19, p < .001) controlling for curiosity itself; people were also more willing to wait for positively valenced trivia (1.31, p < .01), with no effect for negative valence (Caroline Marvin and Daphna Shohamy, "Curiosity and Reward: Valence Predicts Choice and Information Prediction Errors Enhance Learning", Journal of Experimental Psychology: General 145(3), 2016, https://shohamylab.zuckermaninstitute.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/2017-02/Curiosity.pdf — peer-reviewed; single study, N = 84, self-report measures). A hook that inflates curiosity beyond what the payoff can satisfy produces the negative error: in practitioner terms, the clickbait feeling.
Check: the reviewer scores each hook's implied promise 1–5 and the payoff's delivery 1–5; any section where promise > payoff fails. Also check each section's resolving sentence: is the answer stated as something understood, not only as a negation of the misconception?

7. **Voice the common wrong belief and refute it on screen — as a dialogue between a wrong and a right voice, or as a single-speaker refutation; clean exposition leaves viewers feeling they already knew it.**
Muller's design experiments built matched physics videos that differed only in whether common alternative conceptions were voiced and resolved. Across three iterations the Dialogue and Refutation treatments outperformed Exposition and an equal-length Extended Exposition; viewers of the misconception versions reported more mental effort and recalled more, while Exposition viewers "felt the material was review and therefore didn't pay full attention". Confidence rose by a similar amount in every group, so plain exposition raised confidence without raising scores (Derek Muller, "Designing Effective Multimedia for Physics Education", University of Sydney, 2008, https://www.sydney.edu.au/science/physics/pdfs/research/super/PhD(Muller).pdf — PhD thesis, read from the archived copy; the peer-reviewed version is Muller, Bewes, Sharma and Reimann, "Saying the wrong thing: improving learning with multimedia by including misconceptions", Journal of Computer Assisted Learning 24(2), 2008, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2729.2007.00248.x — 364 students randomised across four treatments; full text not retrieved). Two limits: the outcome is conceptual learning, not watch time, and the tested dialogues interleaved misconception and correction throughout, so the evidence says "include and refute", not "open on it"; opening on the misconception is a house choice made in `structure-and-hooks.md` rule 15. The d = 0.71 that circulates for Dialogue vs Exposition was not verified.
Check: the research stage lists the top one to three lay misconceptions for the topic with a source; the plan carries a `misconception` field. In script review, for each key explanation: is a misconception stated in the viewer's voice and then explicitly refuted, not merely omitted? Fail a section that gives only the correct account of something lay viewers commonly get wrong.

## What is at risk

8. **Whenever possible, tell the viewer what is at risk and by when before the mechanism is explained, so the explanation plays as suspense rather than lecture; withhold the danger only when a twist is the point of the film.**
Hitchcock's bomb under the table: a mundane conversation grips only if the audience knows the bomb is there and goes off at one o'clock — "fifteen seconds of surprise" against "fifteen minutes of suspense", with the conclusion that "whenever possible the public must be informed" and an explicit exception for twist endings (Truffaut's 1962 interviews, quoted in David Bordwell, "Hitchcock, Lessing, and the bomb under the table", 2013, https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2013/11/29/hitchcock-lessing-and-the-bomb-under-the-table/ — practitioner essay; also reproduced in Futility Closet, "Hitchcock's Bomb Theory", 2023, https://www.futilitycloset.com/2023/12/20/hitchcocks-bomb-theory/ — secondary). Bordwell adds the general form: widening the audience's knowledge beyond the characters' builds suspense; confining it only produces shared surprise. Verification replaced the original "never" with the source's "whenever possible": Hitchcock broke the rule in Rebecca, Suspicion and Psycho, Bordwell cites critics who think surprise has its own value, and the idea predates him (Lessing, 1767–69). The transfer to factual film is this document's extrapolation; no retention data exists.
Check: the reviewer locates the first sentence that states a concrete loss or danger and its deadline or trigger; it must precede the first mechanism-explaining sentence unless the beat sheet declares a twist structure.

9. **The plan must state the stakes concretely — what happens if the protagonist or system does not succeed — and later beats should put more at risk than earlier ones.**
Pixar's rule 16, from Emma Coats's tweets of rules she picked up from senior colleagues: "What are the stakes? Give us reason to root for the character. What happens if they don't succeed? Stack the odds against." ("Pixar's 22 Rules of Storytelling", Tubefilter, 2012, https://tubefilter.com/2012/06/12/pixar-rules-of-storytelling/ — practitioner; personal tweets, not official guidance). McKee's Story, via a secondary summary, says the protagonist must risk what they care about most and that risk is heightened incrementally over the story (Shortform summary of Robert McKee, Story, https://www.shortform.com/pdf/story-pdf-robert-mckee — secondary; McKee's own text not verified). Verification weakened the original per-beat version: both sources speak at the level of the whole story, and the per-beat "one concrete sentence" test, strict monotonic escalation and the extension of "protagonist" to a grid, a species or the viewer are this project's operationalisation.
Check: PLAN_SCHEMA carries a `failure_consequence` for the film written as a concrete outcome (a noun and a verb; "things get worse" fails); the reviewer confirms at least one later beat raises the consequence above an earlier one. Per-beat consequences are a house convention: a warning, not a fail.

10. **Within the opening, state the consequence for the viewer's own life or for people like them; the film's answer to "why should I care" is addressed to the viewer, not to the subject.**
Transportation into a narrative correlates modestly with perceived personal relevance ("personally relevant" r = .26, "impact on my life" r = .28, N = 274; correlational) (Melanie Green and Timothy Brock, "The Role of Transportation in the Persuasiveness of Public Narratives", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 79(5), 2000, https://gwern.net/docs/www/pdfs.semanticscholar.org/39691cee32e52fbfc33f1a0316aa0d008cb699a4.pdf — peer-reviewed, Exp. 3). Cleo Abram's editorial filter for Huge If True is the practitioner version: "What is it about this thing that could help more people live easier or happier lives?" and, for endings, "why should we participate in that future?" (Levels podcast #195 with Cleo Abram and Ben Grynol, https://www.levels.com/podcasts/195-video-content-creation-and-maintaining-journalistic-integrity-cleo-abram-ben-grynol — practitioner; 08:44 and 47:16). Johnny Harris's "Every Story is a Promise" is attested only as a chapter title of the How I Write episode (David Perell and Johnny Harris, 12 March 2025, https://podcasts.apple.com/bg/podcast/johnny-harris-master-storytelling-on-youtube/id1700171470?i=1000698950686 — practitioner; transcript paywalled), so do not attribute wording to him.
Check: within the first ~90 seconds of narration, the reviewer finds a sentence whose object is the viewer ("you", "your", "people like you", or a named group the audience belongs to) and that states a consequence. The plan carries a `viewer_stake` field; empty fails. The 90-second window is a house choice, not a sourced number.

11. **Anchor the film on one moment of change — a few seconds in which something or someone becomes different — and open on the opposite state; a beat sheet with no such moment has no stakes to pay off.**
Matthew Dicks's craft rule for told stories: every story serves a "five-second moment" of transformation or realisation, the opening is the opposite of that moment, and the four tools for holding attention are stakes, surprise, suspense and humour (Lenny Rachitsky and Matthew Dicks, "How to tell better stories", Lenny's Newsletter, https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-tell-better-stories-matthew — practitioner; chapters 04:27, 14:28, 23:14, 48:46). For a factual film the moment is the discovery, the failure, the decision or the measurement that changed the picture. Practitioner rule with no data, kept because it is a concrete test a reviewer can run.
Check: the plan has a `five_second_moment` field naming who or what changes and when; the reviewer confirms the opening beat describes the pre-change state and that one beat corresponds to the moment. Fail on either miss.

## Stakes through people, then numbers

12. **As a craft default, carry the stakes through one named, pictured person in a specific situation rather than a population statistic, and never present that person as proof of scale; the sympathy effect behind this is contested, not established.**
Small, Loewenstein and Slovic found higher giving toward a single described child than toward statistics ($2.12 vs $1.21, Study 1) but no significant difference on self-reported feelings (Deborah Small, George Loewenstein and Paul Slovic, "Sympathy and callousness: The impact of deliberative thought on donations to identifiable and statistical victims", Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 102(2), 2007, https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/Sympathy_and_callousness.pdf — peer-reviewed). A meta-analysis of 41 effects put the identifiable-victim effect at r = .05, strongest for a single photographed child in poverty with a monetary ask (Seyoung Lee and Thomas Feeley, "The identifiable victim effect: a meta-analytic review", Social Influence 11(3), 2016, https://pure.skku.edu/en/publications/the-identifiable-victim-effect-a-meta-analytic-review/ — peer-reviewed). A bias-adjusted reanalysis estimates the average effect at r = 0.002 (BF01 = 14.93), and a preregistered replication of Small et al. with hypothetical donations found no effect (Maximilian Maier, "Mapping the Moral Mind", UCL, 2025, ch. 2, https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10211774/1/PhD_Thesis_MM.pdf — PhD thesis; the chapter corresponds to Maier, Wong and Feldman 2023, Collabra: Psychology). Nothing shows statistics beat individuals, so the default survives as craft; the "memory" claim in the original has no support in these sources and was dropped.
Check: the stakes beat names at least one specific person or entity with a concrete situation (name or precise description, place, moment), and the first emotional appeal is not a bare aggregate number. The research stage lists the identifiable case and its source.

13. **When the stakes concern several people, present them as one entitative unit — a family shown together, described as one group — rather than as separate individuals; in the lab, feeling and giving were highest for one child and lower for two.**
Västfjäll and colleagues: donations of 37.7 SEK for one child vs 26.3 for two (Study 1a); 24.9 / 23.4 / 21.3 SEK for 1 / 2 / 8 children within subjects (Study 2); eight children described as a family in one photograph drew 32.1 against 20.0 for eight unrelated children (Study 3, t(51) = 2.6) (Daniel Västfjäll, Paul Slovic, Marcus Mayorga and Ellen Peters, "Compassion Fade: Affect and Charity Are Greatest for a Single Child in Need", PLOS ONE 9(6), 2014, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4062481/ — peer-reviewed; Swedish student samples, Study 1a hypothetical). Slovic's chapter gives the correlation form — donation tracked feeling at r = .52 for a single child and r = .19 for two combined — and proposes that emotion "is greatest at N = 1 but begins to decline at N = 2" as a model, not a measured curve (Paul Slovic, David Zionts, Andrew Woods, Ryan Goodman and Derek Jinks, "Psychic Numbing and Mass Atrocity", 2010 preprint of a chapter in The Behavioral Foundations of Policy, https://untref.edu.ar/documentos/ceg/50%20Paul%20Slovic.pdf — book chapter, not journal peer review). Extension to a crew, a village, a named ship, or to narrative stakes in film is extrapolation; a 2024 field study reportedly finds the opposite (see Contested).
Check: the human stakes beat centres on exactly one named individual or one named unit; flag beats that alternate between two or more separate individuals, or that introduce the group only as a count.

14. **Keep the big statistic out of the beat that carries the human case, and do not make the viewer calculate before the emotional beat has landed; widen to scale in a separate, later beat.**
In Small et al.'s Study 3 (n = 159), attaching statistics to the identifiable child's story erased the extra giving the story produced — identifiable plus statistics was indistinguishable from statistics alone (p = 1.0) — and a prime that made people do arithmetic before deciding also cut giving to the individual (Small, Loewenstein and Slovic 2007, URL above; Slovic et al. 2010, URL above, Section 4). Maier's exploratory analysis mirroring Study 3 found no such effect (BF01 = 25.03; Maier 2025, URL above). So this is structural advice with support in one study and a failed replication; it is kept because writers routinely "prove" a stakes beat by piling numbers onto the anecdote, and no evidence says that helps.
Check: within sentences tagged as the stakes or human beat, count numerals ≥ 1,000 and percentages; zero allowed. Scale numbers appear in a later, separately tagged beat. Flag any beat that asks the viewer to compute ("that's X per person per day") before the emotional beat.

15. **Never leave a large number bare: pair every mass-scale figure with a physical image or a comparison at human scale.**
Slovic's psychic-numbing work: numbers of lives are "human beings with the tears dried off"; the affective system responds to images and single cases, and sensitivity to the number itself diminishes as it grows (Slovic et al. 2010, URL above, Sections 3.1 and 4; Paul Slovic, "'If I Look at the Mass I Will Never Act': Psychic Numbing and Genocide", Judgment and Decision Making 2(2), 2007, https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/items/d78e57ac-eb78-481c-9b14-0085d4f26e47 — peer-reviewed, abstract via repository). The device he cites is converting the figure into something seen: 38,000 pairs of shoes piled in front of the Capitol for 38,000 handgun deaths. Heath and Heath package the same point as concreteness (Chip Heath and Dan Heath, Made to Stick, 2007, "Emotional" chapter, via https://www.theologyofwork.org/the-high-calling/emotional/ — book, known only through a blog commentary on it, not an excerpt; page numbers unverified). Effect sizes exist only for donation outcomes, not for video retention. For the pipeline this is a keyframe and VFX obligation as much as a script one.
Check: for each numeral ≥ 1,000 in the script, the same or adjacent sentence contains a comparison ("about the population of…", "enough to fill…") or the keyframe spec for that line depicts the quantity. QA flags bare numbers.

16. **Write the stakes beat as a scene the viewer can picture — a person to identify with, a plot they can imagine, lifelike detail — because transportation into the scene, not the argument's logic, is what moves feeling, attitude and intention.**
A meta-analysis of 132 effect sizes separates what the storyteller controls from receiver traits: identifiable characters (ρ = .20), imaginable plot (ρ = .29) and verisimilitude (ρ = .27) raise transportation; transportation in turn predicts affective responses (ρ = .57), attitudes (.44), intentions (.31), beliefs (.26) and fewer critical thoughts (−.20) (Tom van Laer, Ko de Ruyter, Luca Visconti and Martin Wetzels, "The Extended Transportation-Imagery Model: A Meta-Analysis of the Antecedents and Consequences of Consumers' Narrative Transportation", Journal of Consumer Research 40(5), 2014, https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/1/SSRN-id2033192.pdf — peer-reviewed). Green and Brock showed the mirror: instructing readers to hunt for difficult words lowered transportation and with it story-consistent beliefs (Exp. 4, N = 258; Green and Brock 2000, URL above). Anything that pulls attention to surface features — jargon, meta-commentary, visible errors — reduces transportation and the belief effect with it.
Check: the reviewer scores each stakes beat 0–2 on three items: (a) an agent the viewer can identify with is present, (b) events run as a sequence that can be visualised (who did what, where), (c) sensory or lifelike specifics are present. Beats under 4/6 go back to script.

17. **Choose the dominant emotion of each stakes beat deliberately and prefer high-arousal ones — awe, anger, anxiety; do not build a stakes beat on sadness alone.**
In a field study of about 7,000 New York Times articles plus experiments, sharing was driven by physiological arousal rather than valence: a one-standard-deviation increase in awe raised the odds of making the most-emailed list by 30%, anger by 34%, while sadness lowered them, controlling for surprise, interest, usefulness and placement (Jonah Berger and Katherine Milkman, "What Makes Online Content Viral?", Journal of Marketing Research 49(2), 2012, https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf — peer-reviewed data study). The outcome is sharing, not watch time, so for retention this is a hypothesis for the evidence loop; it is the only large data study of emotion in content that gives effect sizes. Anxiety is also what manufactured stakes exploit; rule 20 bounds it.
Check: each beat in the plan is tagged with one dominant emotion from {awe, anger, anxiety, curiosity, amusement, sadness, calm}. A rule flags any film whose stakes beat is tagged sadness or calm, and any film with no high-arousal beat. `yt improve` later correlates tags with retention.

## Honest stakes

18. **The narrator speaks as the viewer's proxy who is finding out, not as the expert who already knows; expertise is attributed to named sources, and the narrator's curiosity carries the stakes.**
Cleo Abram states the persona directly — "I consider myself a proxy for the audience when I'm in these rooms" (YouTube Official Blog, "5 huge if true moments for Cleo Abram", https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/5-huge-if-true-moments-for-cleo-abram/ — practitioner) — and her integrity rule is to say plainly "I'm not an expert on this. Here's what the top three reports that I've read tell me" (Levels podcast #195, URL above, 49:17). Johnny Harris's "look at this thing!" habit is reported only by a third party (Chengwei Hu, "Visual before Context", https://chengweihu.com/io/visual-before-context/ — secondary). For an AI-written explainer this guards against the lecture register and doubles as an accuracy device: the proxy can be wrong and corrected on screen; the expert voice cannot. Testimony, not data.
Check: the reviewer counts narrator assertions of mechanism in the stakes and explanation beats that carry no attribution; the narrator's own voice should carry questions, observations and attributed claims. Flag scripts where the narrator asserts expert conclusions in the first person without a source.

19. **Do not rely on "this is factual" to carry weight: labelling a story as fact does not raise transportation, transported viewers scrutinise less, so every stakes claim is sourced in research before the script is written.**
Green and Brock found readers equally transported whether the same story was labelled fiction or non-fiction (F < 1), with beliefs unaffected by the label; highly transported readers found fewer "false notes" in the story (Exp. 1 and 2; Green and Brock 2000, URL above). Dahlstrom draws the conclusion for science communication: narratives are intrinsically persuasive, are judged on verisimilitude rather than accuracy, and "can rarely be effectively countered with facts" (Michael Dahlstrom, "Using narratives and storytelling to communicate science with nonexpert audiences", PNAS 111 (Suppl 4), 2014, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4183170 — peer-reviewed). This inverts the usual order: a story-shaped stakes beat is more persuasive and less checked, so the research gate runs before the story gate.
Check: every sentence in a beat tagged `stakes` maps to a research-stage citation id; the script reviewer rejects stakes beats with uncited causal or quantitative claims. Order of gates: research, then script review, then voice.

20. **Manufactured stakes are an accuracy breach, not a style choice: a stakes beat may simplify, but it may not invent a villain, a hidden plot, a deadline or a motive the sources do not support, and any dramatisation used as a device is flagged as such on screen or in narration.**
Dahlstrom's ethics framework asks three questions of a factual narrative: is the purpose comprehension or persuasion; what accuracy and representativeness is kept (choosing a non-representative story is exactly what a persuader would do); and should narrative be used at all (Dahlstrom 2014, URL above, "Ethical considerations"). The Harris case supplies the failure modes: a "quest to uncover a secret plot" as the default frame, evidence nuggets prioritised over context, a Columbus beat later admitted to be dramatised as a "device/symbol", and sponsor-written content disclosed "in the last minute" (Jonathan Jarry, "The Many Mistakes of Johnny Harris", McGill Office for Science and Society, https://www.mcgill.ca/oss/article/critical-thinking-health-and-nutrition/many-mistakes-johnny-harris — practitioner critique). Abram's own test: "I can tell you a false story with a list of accurate facts" (Levels #195, URL above, 38:04). The beat can be wrong when every sentence is true.
Check: per stakes beat the reviewer answers (1) is there an antagonist, motive or deadline, and which source states it; (2) is the chosen case representative or the extreme, and if extreme does the script say so; (3) is any reconstructed or hypothetical scene marked. A different-family reviewer answers Abram's test: could this beat be false while every sentence is true?

## The attention budget: load and clarity

21. **Cut seductive details — interesting-but-irrelevant anecdotes, jokes, inserts, stings and facts that do not serve the explanation — and keep prose coherent at the sentence level.**
Mayer's coherence principle: people learn better when extraneous material is excluded. In a 2025 meta-analysis of 181 studies from Mayer's lab, removing seductive detail and sentence-level coherence are listed among the largest significant effects (Jennifer Cromley and Yang Chen, "A meta-analysis of Richard Mayer's multimedia learning research: Searching for boundary conditions of design principles across multiple media types", Educational Research Review 49, 2025, abstract at https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927 — peer-reviewed; the per-principle values g = 1.00 and 0.63 come from a search snippet of a PDF that could not be opened and should be confirmed against the paper's table). Mayer's own 2017 review gives coherence a median d = 0.70 (Richard Mayer, "Using multimedia for e-learning", Journal of Computer Assisted Learning 33(5), 2017, https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcal.12197 — peer-reviewed; the page returned 403, abstract relayed via search). The "retention trick" of adding tangents, memes or dramatic music has the opposite sign for comprehension; narrative colour that is itself the mechanism is not extraneous.
Check: for each sentence and shot, the reviewer answers "which claim or step does this serve?"; no answer means cut. A rule flags sentences that introduce a named entity, date or anecdote never referenced again; sound and VFX flag stings, memes or inserts tied to no script beat.

22. **Signal structure explicitly — say "there are three reasons" before giving them, label the parts on screen, and highlight the element the narration is about at the moment it is named.**
Cues that mark organisation and the mapping between words and pictures improve learning, especially for low-prior-knowledge audiences: d = 0.38 across 44 effect sizes from 29 experiments and 2,726 participants (David Alpizar, Olusola Adesope and Rachel Wong, "A Meta-Analysis of Signaling Principle in Multimedia Learning Environments", Educational Technology Research and Development 68(5), 2020, https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1269127 — peer-reviewed); Mayer's 2017 median is d = 0.46 (Mayer 2017, URL above; abstract only). Small-to-medium, consistent, and cheap for an animated explainer: verbal signposts in the script, visual emphasis synchronised to narration in keyframe and VFX.
Check: every enumerated explanation states its count before the list. For each narrated noun with an on-screen counterpart, it is visually emphasised when named. A rule requires at least one verbal signpost per section and pre-announced lists.

23. **Narrate over graphics instead of printing the narration; keep on-screen words to short labels and the few numbers a viewer must hold; keep each spoken explanatory unit short, because spoken words vanish.**
Three effects combine. Modality: graphics plus narration beats graphics plus the same words as text (Mayer 2017, median d = 0.72; Cromley and Chen 2025, g = 0.82 per snippet). Redundancy: verbatim captions over narration and graphics hurt in Mayer's 2017 review (d = 0.87), but his own 2021 table gives d = 0.10 in 8 of 12 tests and the 2025 reanalysis of the same lab found "avoiding redundant captions" at g = 0.14, not significant — see Contested and `direction-and-narration.md` rule 2, which holds the keyword-label exception (Mayer and Johnson 2008). Transience: spoken and animated information "disappears after a few seconds", so long verbal passages overload working memory and the modality advantage reverses when the audio is long (John Sweller, Jeroen van Merriënboer and Fred Paas, "Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design: 20 Years Later", Educational Psychology Review 31, 2019, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5 — peer-reviewed; first half of the article read). For TTS narration: one idea per sentence, persistent labels for the few things the viewer must keep in mind, no wall-of-text slides.
Check: flag any on-screen text block over ~8 words that duplicates the narration; flag narration sentences over ~25 words and stretches over ~40 s without a visual anchor; require any number carried across sections to also appear as a persistent label. Reviewer: can the explanation be followed with audio only, and with visuals only? It should need both.

24. **Chunk the film into bounded segments of one mechanism each, with an explicit boundary line, and pre-train the two or three terms a segment depends on before explaining how they interact.**
Segmenting into meaningful units helps even when the system, not the learner, sets the pace, which is the YouTube case: d = 0.32 retention and 0.36 transfer across 88 comparisons, with the system-paced subset at 0.42 and 0.35 (Günter Daniel Rey, Maik Beege, Steve Nebel, Maria Wirzberger, Tobias Schmitt and Sascha Schneider, "A Meta-analysis of the Segmenting Effect", Educational Psychology Review, 2019, https://maria-wirzberger.de/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Rey2019_Article_AMeta-analysisOfTheSegmentingE.pdf — peer-reviewed). Mayer's 2017 medians are higher (segmenting 0.70, pre-training 0.46; URL above), while the 2025 reanalysis of his lab found both non-significant (segmenting g = 0.19, pre-training 0.28, per snippet), so confidence is moderate. Rey found the retention benefit largest for high-prior-knowledge learners (d = 0.73 against 0.29 for none). The natural unit for yt-auto is the section: one open question, one mechanism, one payoff, bounded.
Check: each beat-sheet section names exactly one mechanism and ends on an explicit boundary line. A rule flags sections that introduce more than ~3 new named components before the first causal statement, and any technical term used in a causal sentence that was not defined earlier in the same or a prior section.

25. **Write narration in conversational second person ("you", "we", "let's") and treat a machine-sounding voice as a QA defect.**
Mayer's personalization principle — conversational rather than formal wording of the same lesson — is among the largest effects in his corpus: median d = 0.79 in his 2017 review (URL above; abstract only) and g = 0.70 (95% CI roughly 0.24–1.17) in Cromley and Chen's 2025 reanalysis, where personalization is listed among the "largest significant effects" (abstract confirmed at par.nsf.gov, URL above). The interpretation is social agency: a conversational voice increases the viewer's effort to make sense of the material. The companion voice principle (human over synthetic voice, d = 0.74 in Mayer 2017) predates neural TTS; whether current voices still incur the penalty is unknown (see Folklore and open questions).
Check: ratio of second-person and first-person-plural pronouns to sentences; flag scripts below a threshold or sections written entirely in impersonal passive. Voice QA: a reviewer rates naturalness; flat or robotic prosody on a hook line fails.

26. **Calibrate scaffolding to the audience's prior knowledge: aids that help novices — worked steps, heavy signalling, misconception-first — become neutral or harmful as expertise rises.**
The expertise-reversal effect: instructional procedures that reduce load for novices "can be counterproductive as expertise increases" and can eventually reverse (Sweller, van Merriënboer and Paas 2019, URL above, "Expertise Reversal Effect"). Muller's advanced class showed no Dialogue-vs-Refutation difference while novices needed the misconception treatment (Muller 2008, URL above, section 2.3); Rey et al. found the segmenting benefit for retention varied sharply with prior knowledge (0.29 none, −0.12 some, 0.73 high; URL above). This argues for an explicit target-audience field per film that the reviewer applies when judging density and the misconception-first requirement.
Check: the plan schema carries an audience level (lay / enthusiast / expert); the reviewer checks that defined-term density and misconception handling match it (lay: define everything, misconception-first mandatory; expert: skip basics). Whether the match is right is taste only.

## Measuring attention

27. **Treat the first 30 seconds as the measured gate: by 0:30 the viewer has seen what the title and thumbnail promised and has a live question; after publish, read the Intro percentage, Dips and Top moments and feed them back to the script stage.**
YouTube's analytics define "Intro" as the share of viewers still watching after 30 seconds and attribute a weak intro to a mismatch between the opening and the title and thumbnail; Top moments are where almost no one dropped off, with the advice to "introduce the compelling content earlier"; Spikes are rewatched or shared moments (which can mean unclear content); Dips are skipped or abandoned moments. Key moments need a video of at least 60 s with 100 views, and 1–2 days of processing (YouTube Help, "Measure key moments for audience retention", https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415 — platform documentation). "A typical intro is about 30 seconds long", videos with 50% or more still watching at 0:30 are listed as "above typical intros", and audiences "tend to decrease as videos progress" (YouTube Help, "Content tab analytics tips", https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12942217 — platform documentation). This is the platform's measurement contract, not a psychological threshold, and the empirical hook for the evidence loop: every Dip is a candidate script defect (buried question, seductive detail, overload), every Spike a candidate clarity defect. The ingest schema and the like-for-like comparison rule are `judging-narrative.md` rules 1–3; the writing-side rule is `structure-and-hooks.md` rules 9 and 38. This rule only states what the attention evidence predicts those numbers will show.
Check: at build, compute the timestamp at which the title's promised object first appears in narration or on screen; fail if over 30 s. At improve, ingest the retention curve, map Dips and Spikes to beat-sheet sections, and open findings routed to script with the section id.

28. **Keep sections short and the pace brisk: do not slow the narration for clarity, and do not let a segment run past about six minutes without a boundary.**
In 6.9 million edX sessions, median watch time was at most six minutes regardless of video length, and students often made it less than halfway through videos over nine minutes; within a length band, engagement rose up to 2× with speaking rate across 48–254 wpm (mean 156), with no difference in play/pause events, so not confusion-driven rewatching (Philip Guo, Juho Kim and Rob Rubin, "How Video Production Affects Student Engagement: An Empirical Study of MOOC Videos", L@S 2014, https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf — peer-reviewed data study; correlational). The length finding is about instructional segments in an enrolled-learner population, not total YouTube runtime: a 20-minute film can be six 3-minute segments. The speaking-rate result is directly usable for the voice and retime stages.
Check: words-per-minute of the rendered narration, using the bands in `direction-and-narration.md` rule 25 (warn outside 150–190, fail under 130 or over 200); section durations (flag any section over ~6 minutes between boundaries). Validate both thresholds against the channel's own retention curves once available.

## Contested

**Open loops work because of the Zeigarnik effect.** A 2025 meta-analysis across 38 publications found no memory advantage for interrupted tasks (weighted ratio of interrupted to completed tasks recalled 0.99; dz = 0.15) and concludes the effect "lacks universal validity"; only the Ovsiankina tendency to resume an interrupted task replicated (Ghibellini and Meier, Humanities and Social Sciences Communications 12:962, 2025, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w — peer-reviewed). Open loops are justified by the information-gap mechanism (rule 1), not by a memory effect; no study of open loops on video watch time was found.

**Pattern interrupts give a dopamine hit, so change something every N seconds.** Midbrain dopamine codes reward prediction error, "the simple difference between obtained and predicted reward" (Wolfram Schultz, 2016, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4826767/ — peer-reviewed); novelty contributes only a brief, unselective salience component, though human SN/VTA does respond to absolute novelty (Bunzeck and Düzel, Neuron, 2006, https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/absolute-coding-of-stimulus-novelty-in-the-human-substantia-nigra/ — peer-reviewed), and Gruber et al. say they "cannot make strong conclusions" that their fMRI signal reflects dopamine release. Nothing supports a fixed cadence of visual change, and the seductive-details evidence (rule 21) points the other way for comprehension.

**Plain explanation makes viewers more confident in their misconceptions.** Partly supported: Muller found confidence rose similarly in every treatment while only the misconception versions raised scores. The stronger claim that exposition is worse than no instruction is not what the thesis says; the JCAL abstract implies exposition produced a smaller gain, not none.

**Captions of the narration help retention and accessibility.** Mayer's redundancy principle (d = 0.87 in his 2017 review, d = 0.10 in his 2021 table) says verbatim captions over narration and graphics hurt learning; the 2025 reanalysis of his own lab found g = 0.14, not significant; Sweller's transience work says the modality advantage reverses for long passages; muted mobile viewing is a practical reason for captions that none of these studies address. Unresolved. The safe rule (23) is short labels, not a transcript on screen.

**Curiosity is the main driver of learning from video.** Gruber et al. call the incidental-memory effect "relatively subtle"; Wade and Kidd (2019, not read) reportedly found curiosity tracked self-estimated rather than objective knowledge with only a small learning boost. Curiosity reliably moves attention and willingness to wait or pay (Kang Exp. 3; Marvin and Shohamy); its effect on retention of content is modest. It is a hook mechanism; the load-and-clarity rules (21–26) do the comprehension work.

**Signalling, segmenting and pre-training are settled.** Mayer's 2017 medians (0.46 / 0.70 / 0.46) and Cromley and Chen's 2025 reanalysis of the same lab (segmenting 0.19 n.s., pre-training 0.28 n.s., signalling not retrieved) disagree in magnitude, and Cromley and Chen report effect sizes declining by publication year. Independent meta-analyses (Rey 2019, Alpizar 2020) support small-to-medium effects. Use them; do not expect large retention swings from them alone.

**Keep videos under six minutes; viewers decide in the first 30 seconds.** The six-minute figure is a median engagement time for MOOC lecture segments among enrolled learners, not a YouTube runtime limit. The 30-second mark is YouTube's reporting checkpoint for Intro, and 50% retained there is simply the "above typical" bucket; neither is a psychological threshold found in any study read here.

**Speak at about 160 words per minute.** Guo et al. found engagement rose with speaking rate up to the fastest 254 wpm and trace the 160-wpm rule to a 1967 live-lecture recommendation; faster narration was not associated with more rewinding.

**Curiosity-gap headlines always win.** The Upworthy data show vague headlines lose in most tests and beat concrete ones only when competitors are already concrete; question headlines slightly reduced intention to read (Scacco and Muddiman, via Table 1 of PMC11704130). The information-gap theory predicts exactly this: a gap must be specific to be felt.

**The identifiable victim is a sympathy multiplier.** Lee and Feeley's r = .05; Maier's bias-adjusted r = 0.002 and failed preregistered replication; Byrd and Białek (2021) found no effect of identifying a victim in COVID messaging. Rule 12 keeps the individual as a craft vehicle for comprehension, not as a guaranteed effect.

**Compassion fade holds in the real world.** The lab studies show giving falling from one child to two; a 2024 study of GoFundMe campaigns is titled "Real-World Online Donations Contradict Compassion Fade" (Nonprofit and Voluntary Sector Quarterly, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08997640241255707 — peer-reviewed, not read). The N = 1 vs N = 2 rule (13) is lab-supported, not field-confirmed.

**Statistics cancel the human case (Small et al. Study 3).** Did not replicate in Maier's exploratory analysis (BF01 = 25.03). Rule 14 stands as craft, not as an established effect.

**Made to Stick's Rokia numbers.** The book's $2.38 vs $1.14 do not match the cell means in Small et al. ($2.12 vs $1.21 overall; $2.83 for identifiable with no intervention). Cite the paper, not the book, for numbers.

**Sadness kills retention.** Berger and Milkman measured sharing of NYT articles, not watch time; whether high-arousal emotion raises YouTube retention has no public primary data. Rule 17 is a tagged hypothesis for the evidence loop.

**Hitchcock's bomb theory.** Hitchcock's own films often confine knowledge to the protagonist; the idea predates him (Lessing, 1767–69); twist structures are an explicit exception. A strong heuristic, not a measured effect.

**Stakes must appear in the first 30 seconds.** Circulates on YouTube-advice blogs with no primary data; nothing in YouTube's own documentation mentions stakes. Rule 10's ~90-second window is a house choice.

## Folklore and open questions

- **Mayer's 2017 per-principle medians** (coherence 0.70, signalling 0.46, redundancy 0.87, modality 0.72, segmenting 0.70, pre-training 0.46, personalization 0.79, voice 0.74) were confirmed only from the abstract as relayed by search; the Wiley page returned 403. Status: unreachable primary, kept in rules 21–25 only because an independent meta-analysis or the Cromley and Chen abstract supports each rule's direction. Open the paper's table and the per-principle comparison counts before quoting a number in `work/house.md`.
- **Cromley and Chen 2025 per-principle values** (seductive detail 1.00, modality 0.82, personalization 0.70, coherence 0.63, pre-training 0.28 n.s., segmenting 0.19 n.s., captions 0.14 n.s.) came from a search snippet; the PDF returned 404 and ScienceDirect 403. Only the abstract (which principles were "largest significant") is verified. Retrieve the table, including the signalling and redundancy rows the snippet omitted.
- **Mayer's voice principle vs neural TTS.** The d = 0.74 rests on pre-neural synthetic voices; whether modern voices still incur the penalty is unknown and matters directly for the voice stage. Status: open; not a rule. Ginns, Martin and Marsh (2013), the conversational-style meta-analysis, was not retrieved.
- **Spitzer et al. (2023/2024)** reportedly show the confidence–curiosity inverted-U becomes a negative slope when the information is judged important (most curiosity at low confidence about important things). Not read. If true it strengthens the conditional in rule 3 for high-stakes topics such as health and money.
- **Wade and Kidd (2019)**: curiosity tracked self-estimated rather than objective knowledge, with a small learning boost. Search summary only.
- **Muller's effect sizes.** Muller 2007 (Instructional Science) and the JCAL 2008 full text were not retrieved; the d = 0.71 for Dialogue vs Exposition is unverified. Veritasium's own process videos ("Khan Academy and the Effectiveness of Science Videos", 2011, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eVtCO84MDj8; "The key to effective educational science videos") were located but not transcribed, so no timestamps are cited; the thesis is the primary source for the same claims.
- **Sweller, van Merriënboer and Paas (2019)**: only the first half of the article was read; the transient-information and expertise-reversal quotations come from that half.
- **An Ovsiankina analogue in video** — returning to a paused video, staying to see the ending — is untested. It would be the honest replacement for Zeigarnik-based open-loop claims if a memory or motivation citation is wanted.
- **Scacco and Muddiman, "The Curiosity Effect"** (New Media & Society) was read only via another paper's Table 1; the original design was not checked.
- **"Every story is a promise" / "the viewer as hero" (Johnny Harris).** Attested only through chapter titles of the How I Write episode (50:27 "Every Story is a Promise", 54:06 "Let Me Show You") and a podcast summary; the transcript and the Bright Trip course are paywalled. Status: folklore until quoted. The "detective formula" reading of Harris (EditorDuel) is a third-party analysis with no retention data.
- **StoryBrand's "the customer is the hero, the brand is the guide."** Known only through secondary summaries, no outcome data. Folklore that happens to agree with Abram's proxy statement (rule 18).
- **Heath and Heath's claim that identity appeals beat self-interest appeals** is in the excerpt; no underlying study was traced. Made to Stick page numbers for the Mother Teresa principle were not verified.
- **BBC Editorial Guidelines section 3** (accuracy; labelling reconstructions) and **Ofcom Broadcasting Code rule 2.2** ("factual programmes… must not materially mislead the audience") could not be fetched (bbc.co.uk blocked; ofcom.org.uk 403). They are the obvious regulatory anchors for rule 20 and should be cited once verified.
- **Kogut and Ritov 2005 (singularity effect) replication**, Cambridge JDM 2024, returned 429 on every attempt; its result would sharpen rule 13.
- **Nothing links any of these effects to YouTube retention curves.** The nearest data are MOOC engagement (Guo 2014) and Upworthy click-through. Until the evidence loop runs (retention Dips and Spikes per section, routed by `yt improve`), every effect size above is a prior, not a measurement for this channel.
- **Not attempted for budget reasons**: Sweller 1988; Leahy and Sweller 2011/2016; Bromberg-Martin and Hikosaka 2009 (monkeys pay for advance information); Wittmann et al. 2007 (novelty anticipation and reward); a publisher text-layer copy of Loewenstein 1994 (the byrdseed.com PDF is a scan of the journal pages with a text layer; the CMU copy has none); Vox, Veritasium and Kurzgesagt process sources on "why should you care".
