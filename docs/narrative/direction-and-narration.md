# Direction and narration

How the picture and the voice of a yt-auto film are made to carry one explanation together: what goes in the frame, when it moves, where the cuts land, and how the narration is written and delivered so a listener who cannot rewind understands it the first time. Each rule names its evidence and the check a reviewer or a rule finding would run; the strength stated is the strength the sources support after verification, not the strength the folklore claims.

Scope: the direction and narration dimensions only. Structure, hooks, stakes and endings live in their own files. Where a rule's number is a house convention rather than a sourced threshold, it says so.

Shared rules. This file is the home for on-screen text and redundancy (rule 2), concrete-before-abstract at frame and sentence level (rules 9 and 20; the section-level version is `structure-and-hooks.md` rule 21), words per minute (rule 25; `attention-and-stakes.md` rule 28 defers to it) and the read-aloud gate (rule 29; `judging-narrative.md` rule 15 states what it may not be used for). The misconception-first opening in rule 22 is governed by `structure-and-hooks.md` rule 15 and evidenced in `attention-and-stakes.md` rule 7; the personalisation effect in rule 18 is weighed in `attention-and-stakes.md` rule 25.

## 1. Binding picture to words

1. **Present each narration clause and its picture concurrently, and cut the animation to the narration track rather than to a fixed shot grid.**
   Mayer's temporal contiguity principle is the finding that people learn better when corresponding words and pictures are presented simultaneously rather than successively; his own summary table gives a median d = 1.31, positive in 8 of 8 tests, second only to the multimedia principle itself (Mayer, "The Future of Multimedia Learning", table adapted from Mayer 2021, https://edtechbooks.org/docx/10349 — the author's own summary in a peer-reviewed journal, not a primary experiment). The experiments behind it compare concurrent presentation with narration-then-animation blocks separated by tens of seconds; nothing in the source fixes a sub-second tolerance, so any lag number is a house choice. Kurzgesagt states the production order plainly: "His voiceover provides the timing for the animation team" (Kurzgesagt, "What we do", n.d., https://kurzgesagt.org/what-we-do?visit=videos — practitioner, a studio about-page). Joss Fong describes the writing-side discipline as script and visuals co-written so that each carries part of the meaning (Fong, SciComm Summer #1, 2022, https://opinionscipod.buzzsprout.com/981667/episodes/10682097-scicomm-summer-1-joss-fong-producing-science-videos — practitioner interview).
   Check: for each narration clause, find the shot whose `visual` names the clause's subject; flag if the shot starts more than 1 s after the clause onset (house tolerance) or if the picture of claim N is still up while claim N+1 is spoken.
   Stages: script, vfx, retime

2. **Do not print the narration on screen; on-screen text is labels, numbers and two-to-three-word keywords placed next to the part of the graphic they name.**
   The direction of the redundancy principle is consistent (adding the narrated sentences as text over a narrated graphic does not help) but its size is contested: Mayer's 2021 table gives a median d = 0.10, positive in only 8 of 12 tests, for "printed text added to graphics and narration" (Mayer, table adapted from Mayer 2021, https://edtechbooks.org/docx/10349 — author's summary, peer-reviewed venue), while the 2014 handbook chapter is reported at 16 of 16 and d = 0.86 but was not readable in this research (see Contested). The keyword exception has its own evidence: 2-3 printed words identical to the narration, placed beside the relevant part of the diagram, improved retention (d = 0.47 and 0.70) but not transfer (Mayer & Johnson, "Revising the redundancy principle in multimedia learning", J. Educ. Psychol. 100(2), 2008, https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.100.2.380 — peer-reviewed). All of this measured learning in short lessons, not watch time.
   Check: flag any on-screen text element over 6 words, any element that is a verbatim narration substring longer than 4 words, and any text not attached to a drawn object.
   Stages: script, keyframe, vfx, shot.on_screen_text

3. **Any text the viewer must read stays up at least 0.3 s per word (minimum about 0.85 s), at no more than 17-20 characters per second, and never spans a cut.**
   Subtitle delivery specifications are the only published, enforced numbers for how fast people read text while watching a picture. Netflix allows up to 17 characters per second for adult programmes, 42 characters per line, a minimum event of five-sixths of a second and a maximum of 7 s (Netflix, "English Timed Text Style Guide", 2024, https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977-English-Timed-Text-Style-Guide — platform documentation). The BBC figure of 160-180 words per minute, about 0.33-0.375 s per word with a floor near 0.3 s, was read only through a secondary copy (BBC Subtitling Guidelines via Clevercast, 2023, https://www.clevercast.com/?p=116405 — platform documentation, secondary); the Netflix figures stand on their own.
   Check: for each text element compute words per visible second and characters per second; flag under 0.3 s per word, under 0.85 s total, over 20 cps, or visibility spanning a cut.
   Stages: vfx, build, shot.on_screen_text

4. **Every section of the plan names its anchor visual before the script is written, and the story is scoped to what can be shown; a section with nothing to show is cut or merged.**
   Vox's pitch template asks for the anchor visuals at the pitch stage; if a producer cannot name them "they know that they do not have a video story there", and scope is cropped "where you stop having things to show people" (Fong, SciComm Summer #1, 2022, https://opinionscipod.buzzsprout.com/981667/episodes/10682097-scicomm-summer-1-joss-fong-producing-science-videos — practitioner). Kurzgesagt chooses "the best visual metaphors and ideas for each scene in the sketching phase" before illustration (Kurzgesagt, "What we do", https://kurzgesagt.org/what-we-do?visit=videos — practitioner). Hampe's picture-off test is the inverse check and is kept in Folklore because it was read only through student excerpts.
   Check: `PLAN_SCHEMA` requires `anchor_visual` per section; a reviewer reads only the shot descriptions, with no narration, and must reconstruct the section's claim; flag sections where that fails, and flag any narrated number, comparison or mechanism that no shot depicts.
   Stages: script, keyframe, plan.anchor_visual

## 2. What is in the frame

5. **Cut decorative detail and lean schematic, not realistic; every element serves the process being explained, by being narrated, labelled, or cued.**
   Mayer's coherence principle (exclude extraneous material) carries a median d = 0.86, positive in 18 of 19 tests (Mayer, table adapted from Mayer 2021, https://edtechbooks.org/docx/10349 — author's summary, peer-reviewed venue). Tversky's apprehension principle reaches the same design conclusion from the perception side: "animations should lean toward the schematic and away from the realistic", and "clear understanding is a prerequisite to including only the information essential to the processes to be conveyed and eliminating extraneous but sometimes appealing information" (Tversky, Morrison & Betrancourt, "Animation: can it facilitate?", Int. J. Human-Computer Studies 57, 2002, p. 258, https://www.tc.columbia.edu/faculty/bt2158/faculty-profile/files/_Morrison_Betrancourt_AnimationCanitfacilitate.pdf — peer-reviewed; the passage is a design implication from a review, not an experiment). Tversky endorses arrows and highlighting as a way an element earns its place, so "named in the narration" is not the test; serving the explanation is.
   Check: for each keyframe, list its drawn elements and map each to a noun in the co-timed narration, a label, or a tagged role (frame, cue); flag unmapped elements and any background, particle or ambient layer with no stated role.
   Stages: styles, keyframe, vfx

6. **When a frame holds more than one candidate focal object, add a selection cue (highlight, arrow, zoom, colour pop, or a visible hand drawing the part) synchronised with the narration.**
   Signalling has a small-to-medium, moderated effect: d = 0.38 across 44 effect sizes, 29 studies and 2,726 participants, with the authors noting prior results were mixed (Alpizar, Adesope & Wong, "A meta-analysis of signaling principle in multimedia learning environments", ETR&D 68(5), 2020, https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in/99900601052901842 — peer-reviewed). Cues that say where to look are the supported kind; organisational and relational cueing "requires more consideration" (de Koning, Tabbers, Rikers & Paas, "Towards a framework for attention cueing in instructional animations", Educ. Psychol. Rev. 21, 2009, https://repub.eur.nl/pub/16961 — peer-reviewed). Tversky recommends "annotation, using arrows or highlighting or other devices to direct attention to the critical changes and relations" (Tversky et al. 2002, as above). The hand-drawing evidence shows the visible hand matters, not progressive reveal alone: hand-only drawing beat a finished diagram at d = 0.35 regardless of prior knowledge, and the same reveal without the hand did not (Fiorella, "Effects of Observing the Instructor Draw Diagrams on Learning from Multimedia Lessons", UCSB dissertation, 2015, https://escholarship.org/uc/item/55v7s9t1 — dissertation, not peer-reviewed; the three-experiment subset is Fiorella & Mayer, J. Educ. Psychol. 108, 2016, summarised at https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/1/24-1). No source tests word-level timing or the "more than one object" trigger; those are the house operationalisation.
   Check: for each keyframe with 2+ labelled objects, require a `cue` entry (type, target, timestamp); flag if the cue's timestamp is more than 0.5 s from the first spoken mention of the target, or if no cue exists.
   Stages: keyframe, vfx

7. **Build every diagram in discrete steps keyed to the narration, one new part per beat, instead of showing the finished diagram and talking over it.**
   Three independent supports: Mayer's segmenting principle, median d = 0.67 in 7 of 7 tests (Mayer, table adapted from Mayer 2021, https://edtechbooks.org/docx/10349 — author's summary); Tversky's observation that "many continuous events are conceived of as sequences of discrete steps" (Tversky et al. 2002, abstract, as above — peer-reviewed); and Heer & Robertson's guideline "Use staging for complex transitions ... break up the transition into a set of simple sub-transitions", with their Experiment 1 finding that staged animation outperformed plain animation in most conditions (Heer & Robertson, "Animated Transitions in Statistical Data Graphics", IEEE TVCG 13(6), 2007, §3.2.2 and Fig. 6, https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2007-AnimatedTransitions-InfoVis.pdf — peer-reviewed). Primer's format is the same move: simulations where concepts "emerge from simple rules" (Primer store page, https://store.dftba.com/collections/primer — practitioner, a product blurb).
   Check: for any diagram with N named parts, require N build steps whose timestamps are monotonically keyed to the first narration mention of each part; flag a diagram that appears fully formed more than one beat before its parts are named.
   Stages: keyframe, vfx

8. **A shot introduces at most one new claim and at most one group of simultaneous motion, so the viewer never tracks two unrelated changes at once.**
   The phrase "one idea per shot" has no primary source (see Folklore); the rule is the consequence of two that do. Disney's staging principle is "the presentation of any idea so that it is completely and unmistakably clear" (Thomas & Johnston, The Illusion of Life, 1981, p. 53, via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation — book, cited through a secondary page). Heer & Robertson's apprehension guidelines say "Complicated transforms with unpredictable motion paths or multiple simultaneous changes result in increased cognitive load" and that objects undergoing similar changes are perceptually grouped, so grouped motion counts as one change (Heer & Robertson 2007, §3.2.2, as above — peer-reviewed). Fong's dislike of "This is... / Look at this" writing is the script-side symptom: if the line has to point, the shot carries too much (Fong, The Open Notebook, 2020, https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/ — practitioner interview).
   Check: each shot in the plan carries exactly one `claim`; flag shots whose narration span contains two independent claims. In vfx, count distinct animated element groups moving in the same 500 ms window; flag more than one unless tagged as a common-fate group.
   Stages: script, vfx, shot.claim

9. **For every abstract claim, the first shot is a specific worked example, simulation or drawing; the definition or rule comes after the viewer has seen it happen.**
   Grant Sanderson's two most repeated craft rules are "Concrete before abstract" and "Never start with definitions", with "Always ask what picture or visual you could use to elucidate a topic" (Sanderson, 3Blue1Brown About page, SoME talk takeaways, 2021, https://www.3blue1brown.com/about/ — practitioner), and "When we put the visuals first, then start to articulate the meaning, you can get a sense of ownership over it" (The Stanford Daily, 2020, https://stanforddaily.com/2020/01/24/3blue1brown-creator-grant-sanderson-15-talks-engaging-with-math-using-stories-and-visuals/ — practitioner, interview). Fong withheld the black-hole photo until the viewer understood the difficulty: "I wanted to get people to understand why this is so difficult before they saw the result" (Fong, The Open Notebook, 2020, as above). The learning-side support is the dynamic-drawing result in rule 6; the narration-side version is rule 20.
   Check: tag each plan beat as example, mechanism, definition or generalisation; flag any definition or generalisation beat not preceded within the same section by an example or mechanism beat with a concrete shot.
   Stages: script, keyframe, vfx

## 3. Motion and transitions

10. **Any change that carries meaning is slow enough to be perceived: diagram transitions take roughly 0.5-1.5 s with ease-in/ease-out, UI-style moves 150-400 ms, and no meaningful state change happens under about 300 ms.**
    Tversky's apprehension principle: "animations must be slow and clear enough for observers to perceive movements, changes, and their timing" (Tversky et al. 2002, p. 258, as above — peer-reviewed). Heer & Robertson translate it into timing: "Maximize predictability", which "suggests slow-in slow-out timing"; "Make transitions as long as needed, but no longer"; and prior work "recommend[s] transition times around 1 second" for data graphics (Heer & Robertson 2007, §3.2.2, as above — peer-reviewed). The lower band comes from interface guidance: mobile transitions around 300 ms, entering 225 ms, leaving 195 ms, and "Transitions that exceed 400ms may feel too slow" (Google, Material Design "Motion: Duration & easing", 2016, https://m1.material.io/motion/duration-easing.html — platform documentation).
    Check: lint every tween: duration between 150 ms and 1500 ms, easing not linear, and any tween tagged `meaning` (reveals, morphs, value changes) at least 500 ms; flag linear easing and sub-300 ms meaning changes.
    Stages: vfx

11. **When a diagram changes state, morph it with object constancy (the same mark stays the same referent; axes change before values) or cut and re-cue; never reuse a mark for a different thing or change axes and values at once.**
    Heer & Robertson's congruence guidelines: intermediate states remain valid graphics, "marks representing specific data points should not be reused to depict different data points across a transition", and "separating axis rescaling from value changes may help". Their experiments found animated transitions beat static cuts on tracking and estimation across all conditions, and staged animation was preferred to plain animation, with one exception: in the donut-chart condition plain animation beat staged (p = 0.024), so staging is not universal (Heer & Robertson 2007, §3.2.1, Figs. 6-8, https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2007-AnimatedTransitions-InfoVis.pdf — peer-reviewed).
    Check: for consecutive keyframes sharing element ids, require each id to keep its label; require axis or scale changes in a separate tween from value changes; flag id reuse with a changed label and simultaneous axis-plus-value tweens.
    Stages: keyframe, vfx

12. **Keep the picture moving with the information: no static hold longer than about 8 s while the voice continues, and vary shot length so dense passages get more cuts or builds and simple passages fewer.**
    Two literatures point the same way without giving explainer norms. Hollywood average shot length fell from about 10 s in the 1930s-40s to under 4 s after 2000, and the authors argue short shots force gaze reorientation and raise arousal (Cutting, Brunick, DeLong, Iricinschi & Candan, "Quicker, faster, darker: Changes in Hollywood film over 75 years", i-Perception 2(6), 2011, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/ — peer-reviewed; features, not explainers). In 6.9 million MOOC sessions, tablet-drawing tutorials with "motion and continuous visual flow" held viewers 1.5-2x as long as slides (Guo, Kim & Rubin, "How Video Production Affects Student Engagement", L@S 2014, https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf — data study, correlational). Murch ranks rhythm third of six cut criteria, after emotion and story (Murch via No Film School, 2016, https://nofilmschool.com/2016/11/6-rules-good-cutting-according-oscar-winning-editor-walter-murch — practitioner, secondary). The 8 s figure is a house bound.
    Check: from the keyframe/vfx timeline compute per-shot durations and the longest interval with no visual change; flag any no-change interval over 8 s while narration runs, flag an opening 30 s whose change rate is below the film median, and flag a coefficient of variation of shot length under 0.3 (metronomic cutting).
    Stages: keyframe, vfx, qa

## 4. Cuts

13. **Place cuts at clause boundaries and open the incoming shot on motion, so the cut itself goes unnoticed.**
    Murch's thesis is that a cut is a blink punctuating ideas and should land "just before the blink" at the moment the listener gets the idea (Murch, In the Blink of an Eye, via Musicbed, n.d., https://musicbed.com/blog/filmmaking/editing/editing-secrets-from-legendary-editor-walter-murch — practitioner, secondary). Smith & Henderson measured the live-action counterpart with eye tracking: "A quarter of edits joining two viewpoints of the same scene were undetected", about a third when the cut coincided with a sudden onset of motion, and continuity-rule edits were missed more than rule-breaking ones, mostly through "inattentional blindness as viewers attend to the depicted narrative" (Smith & Henderson, "Edit Blindness", J. Eye Movement Research 2(2):6, 2008, https://bop.unibe.ch/JEMR/article/view/2264 — peer-reviewed; Smith, PhD thesis, 2006, https://era.ed.ac.uk/handle/1842/1076). The transfer to animation is untested and a later study reportedly found edit blindness mostly disappears without audio (see Contested).
    Check: align cut timestamps to the narration's clause boundaries from the voice alignment; flag cuts landing mid-clause (more than 0.3 s from a boundary) and incoming shots whose first 500 ms contain no tween.
    Stages: vfx, build

14. **Preserve eye-trace across cuts: the incoming focal object sits where the viewer's eye was, or is cued there within the first beat; when a cut must give something up, give up spatial continuity before rhythm, rhythm before story, story before emotion.**
    Murch's Rule of Six orders what a cut must satisfy (emotion, story, rhythm, eye-trace, two-dimensional plane, three-dimensional space) and says to sacrifice from the bottom: "don't ever give up emotion before story" (Murch via No Film School, 2016, https://nofilmschool.com/2016/11/6-rules-good-cutting-according-oscar-winning-editor-walter-murch — practitioner, secondary). Eye-trace is the one an animator controls completely, since there is no footage constraint. The percentages attached to the list are folklore-grade (see Folklore); the ordering is the rule.
    Check: each keyframe records a focal-point coordinate (screen thirds); flag consecutive shots where the incoming focal point is in a different third from the outgoing one and no cue tween starts within 300 ms.
    Stages: keyframe, vfx

15. **Motivate every cut: a new shot is justified by a new referent, a reaction, a scale change (wide to detail as the narration moves from context to mechanism) or a time jump; unmotivated cuts are removed.**
    Bordwell's account of continuity editing is that a cut delivers what the current frame cannot: "The blockage motivates cutting to Margaret for her reactions", and the "gradual enlargement of the figures" as a sequence intensifies became the standard analytical move (Bordwell, "Rio Jim, in discrete fragments", 2008, https://davidbordwell.net/blog/?p=2590 — practitioner essay by a film scholar). The intensified-continuity default of contemporary style (long-lens masters, extreme close-ups, quick cutting) is described second-hand (Orr, Senses of Cinema review of Bordwell, 2007, https://www.sensesofcinema.com/?p=2181 — secondary). For explainers this gives the overview-then-detail grammar: the whole system first, then cut in as each part is discussed. Confidence is low; this is craft description, not measurement.
    Check: each cut in the keyframe plan carries a motivation tag from {new_referent, reaction, scale_in, scale_out, time_jump, re_hook}; flag untagged cuts, two consecutive scale_in cuts with no new referent, and a section whose first shot is a detail before the system overview has appeared.
    Stages: keyframe, vfx

## 5. Writing for the ear

16. **Keep narration sentences short: aim for 15-20 words, flag anything over 25 words or anything a narrator cannot say in one breath (about 10 s at 150 wpm).**
    Listeners cannot re-read, so NPR's training guide says "In radio writing, sentences need to be far shorter and information needs to unfurl more slowly", and demonstrates by splitting a 38-word newspaper lead into four sentences of 18, 13, 9 and 9 words; it also estimates 37 words at "about 15 seconds to say", which is the 150 wpm pacing assumed here (MacAdam, "The journey from print to radio storytelling", NPR Training, 2017, https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/30/g-s1-65814/the-journey-from-print-to-radio-storytelling-a-guide-for-navigating-a-new-landscape — practitioner). NPR gives no numeric cap; the 25-word maximum is from a training manual (Podger, "Script Writing for Audio Stories", Mobile Journalism Manual, Konrad-Adenauer-Stiftung, https://www.mojo-manual.org/storytelling/audio-storytelling/scriptwriting-for-audio-stories/ — practitioner) and the 15-20 target from an uncredited guide (Journalism University, "Crafting the Perfect Radio Script", 2025, https://journalism.university/broadcast-and-online-journalism/crafting-perfect-radio-script-presenters/ — practitioner, weakest source). NPR's own rewrites average about 12 words, so 15-20 is above the practice it models, not below.
    Check: tokenise narration into sentences; warn over 20 words, fail over 25. After TTS, flag any sentence whose rendered duration exceeds 10 s.
    Stages: script, voice

17. **Keep the subject close to its verb; when "which", "that" or "who" opens a subordinate clause, end the sentence before it and start a new one.**
    NPR calls dependent clauses "toxic to radio writing" and measures the cost: in one obituary lead the subject and verb "are separated by 37 words. Those words would take a reporter or host about 15 seconds to say"; the summary is hedged, "in general, when writing for radio, it's best to keep subjects cozy with their verbs" (MacAdam, NPR Training, 2017, as above — practitioner). David Candow's mechanical version: "The words which or that are strong indicators that you are about to write a subordinate clause. Put a full stop in front of them, and begin a new sentence", and a mid-sentence conjunction "indicates you are linking two thoughts" (Candow, via Sukys, "It's All About Tone", workshop notes 2004-2011, https://megansukys.com/its-all-about-tone/ — practitioner, secondhand notes). Both sources say "beware" and "strong indicators", not "forbid"; a conjunction is a candidate split, not an automatic one.
    Check: flag any sentence containing a relative " which ", " that " or " who " after a noun, or ", and" / ", but" joining two finite clauses; measure token distance from the first subject noun to its finite verb and flag over 10 tokens; count clauses per sentence (target 1, fail over 2).
    Stages: script

18. **Say the line before writing it; reject any word or construction the narrator would not say to one person, and allow fragments and colloquial phrasing.**
    NPR's mantra is "If you wouldn't say it that way, don't write it that way": "Would you say a policy is 'long-disputed' or a country is 'war-torn'? Do you use the verb 'hasten' much? Do you call people 'individuals' or 'residents'?"; "we never put attribution after a quote"; sentence fragments and colloquialisms "can — and should — show up in radio scripts" (MacAdam, NPR Training, 2017, as above — practitioner). Alex Chadwick's "Big Three" are shorter is better, write as you speak, plain and straight, with Ed Bliss's list of words to discard: "effectuate, prioritize, ancillary and utilize" (Chadwick, "Writing for Radio", Transom, 2015, https://transom.org/2015/alex-chadwick/ — practitioner). Nominalisations and contractions are house extensions not stated in either source. The learning-outcome evidence for conversational style (Mayer's personalisation principle) is mixed: the Cromley and Chen 2025 abstract lists personalisation among the largest significant effects in Mayer's lab, but the magnitudes (d ≈ 0.79, g ≈ 0.70) were seen only in snippets, and Lindhaus et al. 2024 found no comprehension or transfer gain in two experiments (see Contested and `attention-and-stakes.md` rule 25). The rule therefore rests on craft, with the learning evidence as support for direction, not size.
    Check: a banned-word list (print journalese, Latinate verbs, "individuals", "residents", "utilize"), a regex for attribution after a quote, and a contraction ratio (expect over 30% of eligible "is not / do not / it is" forms contracted). Reviewer: would the host say this sentence to one friend?
    Stages: script

19. **Use active, subject-first sentences; keep passives and object-first constructions under 10% of lines and never use them for the sentence that carries the section's claim.**
    Auditory comprehension studies show non-canonical word order costs accuracy and effort even when every word is audible: 20 normal-hearing adults scored 88-93% on subject-first sentences and 57-58% on object-first ones, with pupil dilation rising with syntactic complexity rather than noise (Wendt, Dau & Hjortkjær, "Impact of Background Noise and Sentence Complexity on Processing Demands during Sentence Comprehension", Frontiers in Psychology 7:345, 2016, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4785151/ — peer-reviewed). Older adults with hearing loss did worse on passives than actives at equal length, and four-phrase sentences produced more errors than three-phrase ones in both groups (Shin, Noh, Park & Sung, Frontiers in Psychology 14:1264994, 2023, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10641445/ — peer-reviewed). Broadcast style states it as "the police arrested the man", not "the man was arrested by the police" (Media Helping Media, "Writing a radio news script", https://mediahelpingmedia.org/quick-guides/writing-a-radio-news-script/ — practitioner training guide; its guides are reported as partly AI-assisted, so this is a convention statement, not a style authority). The 10% threshold is a house convention.
    Check: passive detector (be-verb plus past participle, optional "by") gives the passive ratio; fail over 10%; flag any passive in a line tagged as a section claim or payoff.
    Stages: script

20. **Put the concrete, picturable instance before the general principle inside the sentence and the beat, and let verbs carry the picture rather than adjectives.**
    Concrete words are recognised and recalled better than abstract ones (Fliessbach et al., "The effect of word concreteness on recognition memory", NeuroImage, 2006, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16861011/ — peer-reviewed, abstract only; Richardson, "Dual coding versus relational processing in memory for concrete and abstract words", Eur. J. Cognitive Psychology 15(4), 2003, https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09541440244000256 — peer-reviewed, abstract only). Radio practice says the same: "Adjectives are paint by number. Verbs allow the listener to paint their own picture" (Candow via Sukys, 2010 note, as above — practitioner); "if you're actually just explaining it, you've already lost ... I have to hand you the paintbrush and you finish the picture" (Abumrad, Twenty Thousand Hertz, https://www.20k.org/episodes/jad-abumrad — practitioner interview). Sentence-level concreteness work (Sadoski, Goetz & Fritz 1993) was not readable and is in Folklore.
    Check: for each sentence containing a nominalisation (-tion, -ity, -ness, -ism) or a word from a "principle" list, require a concrete noun or a number with a unit in one of the two preceding sentences of the same shot or section; warn when the adjective-to-verb ratio per section exceeds 0.6. Reviewer: what does the viewer see when this line is said?
    Stages: script

21. **Say numbers the way a person says them: approximate unless the exact figure is the point, no two figures in one sentence that make the listener do arithmetic, spelled out so the read does not stumble, and few.**
    NPR lists why numbers fail on air: "Numbers can require your listener to do math, which distracts from your story (i.e. 'State A spends $135 million dollars, but State B only spends $70 million')"; "Listeners can't see the numbers, so they are more easily missed, misheard or forgotten"; "There isn't enough time in many radio stories to provide the necessary context for multiple statistics" (MacAdam, NPR Training, 2017, sidebar, as above — practitioner). The rounding convention: "Instead of saying '1,988 people', say 'nearly two thousand people'" (Media Helping Media, as above — practitioner guide, reported as partly AI-assisted; these are training guides, not a broadcaster's style book). Spelling out: "Write out numbers and abbreviations – it will slow your read down if you have to figure out how to say 1,459" (Gilson, "Writing a Radio Script", B-Side Radio, http://bsideradio.org/writing-a-radio-script/ — practitioner). No source gives a significant-figure rule or a per-minute cap; those thresholds below are house.
    Check: count numerals per 60 s of narration (warn over 3); flag any number with more than 2 significant figures not marked `exact`; flag two numerals in one sentence; for TTS, require digits spelled out (years, large numbers, fractions, units) or wrapped in the engine's say-as markup.
    Stages: script, voice

22. **Open the narration on the question the film will answer, not on the facts; do not front-load who, what, when, where and why, and spread the nut across the film.**
    NPR: "The inverted pyramid is the death of a radio story ... Putting who, what, when, where and why into an opening sentence or two is simply not good composition for the ear. It's too much information, too quickly"; the best radio stories "raise a question in listeners' minds at the beginning and promise an answer by the end" (MacAdam, NPR Training, 2017, sections "Abandon the inverted pyramid" and "De-graf the nut graf", as above — practitioner). Derek Muller's variant is to engage prior knowledge first: videos should "engage with people's prior knowledge, which includes talking about misconceptions" (Fritts, "Inside Veritasium", Creator Handbook, 2017, https://www.creatorhandbook.net/inside-veritasium-an-interview-with-dr-derek-muller-12ad2351a4b3/ — practitioner interview) and "Some level of discomfort seems to be essential ... That's when learning can really take place" (Bechard, Scientific American, 2025, https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-youtube-star-derek-muller-of-veritasium-is-challenging-scientific/ — journalism). The structural evidence for the misconception-first opening lives in the structure file; here it governs the first lines of narration.
    Check: the first three sentences of narration contain a question mark or an explicit why/how framing and no more than two of the five Ws; the plan's `promise` is answered in the final section (reviewer matches the payoff against the last section's lines).
    Stages: script, plan.promise

23. **Vary sentence openings and track lengths: no two consecutive narration lines start with the same word or structure, and consecutive tracks between visuals or sound differ in length.**
    "Use tracks of varying length. A series of similar-length tracks becomes monotonous" (Gilson, B-Side Radio, http://bsideradio.org/writing-a-radio-script/ — practitioner); Chadwick's "Freshen the Lingo" asks for clichés and journalese to go (Chadwick, Transom, 2015, https://transom.org/2015/alex-chadwick/ — practitioner). No comprehension data; listed because it is cheap to enforce and the polish prompt already asks for it.
    Check: compare the first two tokens of consecutive sentences (fail on match); coefficient of variation of sentence length per section (warn under 0.25); cliché list.
    Stages: script, sound

24. **In voice-over, clarity beats cleverness: no sentence asks the viewer to process a figure of speech and a new visual at once; pointing lines ("this is...", "look at this") are allowed; and a separate script editor marks every point where they were confused.**
    Joss Fong on the grammar of narrated explainers: "you can't really afford to be too clever or beautiful when you're doing voice-over, because of the sheer amount of information that people are taking in with their eyes and ears"; lines a prose writer "would never do" are correct in video because they bind words to picture; "There's a really high risk of charging forward and not realizing that the viewer is not with you"; every Vox video gets a script editor, and the valuable note is "I was confused at this part" (Fong, The Open Notebook, 2020, https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/ — practitioner interview, single source, so confidence is low).
    Check: a reviewer of a different model family reads each shot's line against its picture description and marks "confused here" or "two new things at once"; mechanical proxy: flag sentences with a metaphor or simile marker ("like a", "as if") that coincide with a shot introducing a new on-screen element.
    Stages: script, keyframe

## 6. Delivery

25. **Budget narration at 150-190 words per minute of voiced time; do not slow the narrator down for clarity, fix comprehension with picture, and never exceed about 210.**
    The one empirical rate study on spoken news found 150 wpm rated slow, 170-190 normal and 210 fast, with the best recall at 170-190; at 230 "the listener is incapable of maintaining and processing the incoming information" (Rodero, "Influence of Speech Rate and Information Density on Recognition", Media Psychology 19(2), 2016, https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15213269.2014.1002942 — peer-reviewed, read through Journalist's Resource, "Radio news: How the pace, density of broadcasts affect what audiences understand, remember", 2015, https://journalistsresource.org/media/radio-news-pace-words/ — data-study summary). Time-compression work puts the comprehension knee near 275 wpm and listeners' self-selected rate at 212 wpm (Arons, "Techniques, Perception, and Applications of Time-Compressed Speech", AVIOS 1992, https://www.media.mit.edu/speech/papers/1992/arons_AVIOS92_timecompressed_speech.pdf — peer-reviewed review). In 6.9 million MOOC sessions "engagement usually increases (up to 2x) with speaking rate" across 48-254 wpm, though the authors note it "might also be due to students getting confused and re-playing parts" (Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014, as above — data study, correlational). The pipeline's `DEFAULT_WPM=150` is at the slow edge; vendor bands of 130-150 for documentary cite no data.
    Check: words divided by voiced seconds, excluding silences over 1 s, times 60 gives effective wpm per section; fail under 130 or over 200, warn outside 150-190; bound voice `speed` so base rate times speed stays under 200; flag sections under 140 wpm or with inserted pauses over 1.5 s not tagged as deliberate beats.
    Stages: script, voice, retime

26. **Trade pace against density: where a passage carries many new terms, names or numbers, slow the read or thin the facts; never deliver dense material fast or thin material slowly.**
    Rodero's "moderate dynamic mechanism" is the only result linking script density to delivery rate: "Low density news items at 150 words per minute and high density items at 210 words per minute were the most difficult to understand"; "The higher the speed, the lower the information density should be and vice versa" (Rodero 2016 via Journalist's Resource 2015, as above). Her audio-ads follow-up reportedly finds about 180 wpm optimal and fast dense delivery ineffective, but was not readable (Rodero, "Do Your Ads Talk Too Fast To Your Audio Audience?", 2020, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/336819723 — peer-reviewed, unread). Arons notes read speech runs faster than spontaneous speech, so a scripted read drifts fast by default.
    Check: density = numerals + capitalised proper nouns + first-occurrence technical terms per 100 words, per section; if a section is in the film's top density quartile, require its effective wpm at or under 160; flag any section over 8 per 100 words for a rewrite.
    Stages: script, voice, retime

27. **Pauses go only at clause and sentence boundaries, a pause precedes the sentence's key term or number, and the edit does not strip those silences.**
    Pauses are processing time, but only in the right place: Reich found pauses improved recall "particularly under conditions of cognitive complexity" yet "are only useful when they occur between clauses within sentences — pauses within clauses are disrupting", and a 330 ms pause inserted ungrammatically added 2 s to response time (Reich 1980 as quoted in Arons 1992, §9.4, as above — peer-reviewed review). Re-inserting silent pauses "at linguistically salient points" into time-compressed narrative "returns recall accuracy to a level approximating that for a normal speech rate" (O'Leary et al., "Strategic Pauses Relieve Listeners from the Effort of Listening to Fast Speech", Trends in Hearing 27, 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37941344/ — peer-reviewed). Filled pauses improve recall while coughs of equal length do not, so the effect is attentional as much as temporal (Fraundorf & Watson, "The disfluent discourse", J. Memory & Language 65, 2011, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749596X11000234 — peer-reviewed). Pauses between every word sound "quite unnatural" and raise cortical tracking without evidence of better understanding (Deoisres et al., PLoS One 18(7), 2023, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10374040/ — peer-reviewed).
    Check: script: no comma inside a clause except lists; every intended beat is a sentence end, a line break or an explicit pause mark. Voice/retime: detect silences over 300 ms in the rendered VO and check each aligns with a sentence or clause boundary from word-level timestamps; flag mid-clause gaps and sentence boundaries with under 150 ms. Sound/build: forbid silence-removal passes that cut boundary pauses below 200 ms.
    Stages: script, voice, retime, sound, build

28. **The script carries delivery marks that survive to the voice stage: one emphasised word per sentence, a pronouncer with the stressed syllable for every name or term whose pronunciation is not obvious, and timings for every non-narration element.**
    NPR's script format shows the conventions: capitals mark the words to stress ("Notice the capitalized phrase 'REGULAR ORDER.' The reporter is indicating that the person reading the intro should emphasize those words"); pronouncers are "critical for any names for which the pronunciation is not obvious. Write out the word phonetically and note which syllable should be stressed"; durations are marked inline (MacAdam, "What does a radio script look like?", NPR Training, 2015, https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/31/g-s1-65406/what-does-a-radio-script-look-like — practitioner). Candow: "Tone is as important as script"; "Words don't carry a message! Only TONE tells the listener what it means" (Candow via Sukys, May 2004 notes, as above — practitioner). For a TTS pipeline these map onto emphasis, phoneme and break controls and must live in the script JSON, not in plain text.
    Check: schema fields per shot: `emphasis` (at most one token per sentence, must be a word in the line), `pronouncers` (required for every capitalised proper noun not in a common-names list); the voice stage maps them to the engine's markup; fail the voice stage if a proper noun lacks a pronouncer or an emphasis mark points at a word not in the line.
    Stages: script, voice, shot.emphasis, shot.pronouncers

29. **Gate the script with a read-aloud before any voice is paid for: render a draft TTS or have a human read it, rewrite every line that stumbles, runs out of breath, repeats a number as if new or buries its point, then listen again.**
    Every practitioner source treats the ear as the test: "Try reading this paragraph out loud and see if you run out of breath. Better yet, read it out loud to a friend or colleague and ask, 'Did you get all that?'" and "the editor judges with his/her ears" (MacAdam, NPR Training, 2017, as above — practitioner). Bob Edwards names the common failure as "failure to read one's own copy aloud"; Noah Adams: "Radio writers fix this by reading their copy aloud" (Chadwick, Transom, 2015, as above — practitioner). Radiolab's method is the extreme form: a hybrid beat-list script ad-libbed on tape, cut, listened to and re-recorded "three or four times", with technical passages read word for word (Glass, "Radiolab: An Appreciation", Transom, 2011, https://transom.org/2011/ira-glass-radiolab-appreciation/ — practitioner). Reading aloud catches surface errors; it is not a test of story (that belongs to the narrative rubric; `judging-narrative.md` rule 15 gives the proofreading evidence and bars story criteria from this pass). "Buries its point" above means a sentence whose key term lands after a subordinate clause, a surface fault, not a judgment of the beat.
    Check: a `yt script --review` step renders a cheap TTS pass, aligns words, and emits per-sentence duration, wpm, pause positions and tongue-twister heuristics (consonant-cluster density, repeated phonemes); any sentence over 10 s or any repeated numeral is a fail. Reviewer listens, not reads, and marks "did you get all that?" points.
    Stages: script, voice

## Contested

- **Redundancy effect size.** Mayer's 2021 table gives d = 0.10 (8 of 12) for printed text added to narration and graphics; the 2014 handbook chapter is reported at d = 0.86 (16 of 16) but returned HTTP 429 on every attempt. Direction is consistent (transcripts on screen do not help), magnitude is not. Short keyword labels are a signalling aid with their own positive evidence (rule 2). Muted mobile viewing adds a practical reason for captions none of these studies address; the safe rule is labels, not transcript.
- **"Animation beats static graphics."** Tversky, Morrison & Betrancourt (2002) found apparent wins for animation came from the animated version carrying more information or interactivity; equivalent static diagrams with arrows did as well. Animation earns its place for continuous change, microsteps and timing, and only when slow, schematic and cued (rules 5, 7, 10).
- **"Staging always beats direct animation."** Heer & Robertson found staged transitions better or preferred in most conditions but significantly worse in the donut-chart condition; staging helps when axis and value changes would otherwise overlap, not universally (rule 11).
- **Murch's Rule of Six percentages** (51/23/10/7/5/4). Only the 51% and the ordering were verifiable; Murch presents the weights as rough. Use the ordering (rule 14).
- **"One idea per shot."** No primary source; a practitioner paraphrase of Disney staging, Mayer segmenting and Heer & Robertson's simple-transition guideline. Confidence in the underlying principles is high, in the phrase low (rule 8).
- **Shot-length figures and the 1/f pattern.** Cutting's 10 s to under 4 s describes Hollywood features; the finding that modern films approach 1/f shot-length variation is sometimes sold as a recipe, which Barry Salt and Cutting himself reject. No shot-length data for animated explainers exists (rule 12).
- **Edit blindness in animation.** Smith & Henderson's data are live-action excerpts with eye tracking; whether animated cuts hide the same way is untested, and a later study (Smith & Santacreu 2017, per search summary) found edit blindness mostly disappeared without audio, so the effect also depends on the soundtrack (rule 13).
- **"Faster speech is more engaging."** Guo et al.'s correlation may partly reflect viewers replaying confusing passages, as the authors say. The safe rule is "do not artificially slow down", not "speed up" (rule 25).
- **"Speak at 160 wpm" and "documentary narration is 120-150 wpm."** Guo et al. attribute the 160 figure to a 1967 live-lecture recommendation; vendor calculators give 130-150 for documentary with no measurement; Rodero's listeners rated 150 as slow. The pipeline's 150 default is defensible for a music-heavy read but at the slow edge of what was best remembered (rule 25).
- **"Remove all silences" / "pauses always help."** Both wrong as stated: boundary pauses aid recall of fast or degraded speech; within-clause pauses harm (+2 s response time for a misplaced 330 ms pause); word-by-word pauses sound unnatural with no demonstrated gain. In cochlear-implant users the benefit appeared only in listeners with better working memory (rule 27).
- **Conversational style improves learning (Mayer personalisation, d ≈ 0.79 / g ≈ 0.70).** The direction is confirmed by the Cromley and Chen 2025 abstract ("largest significant effects"), the figures only by snippets of paywalled pages; Lindhaus et al. (Frontiers in Psychology, 2024, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1405308/xml — peer-reviewed) found no comprehension or transfer gain from personalised written text in two experiments (N = 66 and 77). The direction (conversational is not worse) is safe; the magnitude is not (rule 18).
- **Mayer's voice principle (human beats machine voice, d ≈ 0.74).** Rests on pre-neural TTS; whether current synthetic voices incur the penalty is untested, so it is not an argument against the pipeline's TTS. Treat "sounds synthetic" as a QA judgement, not a sourced effect.
- **"Our ears can only handle one fact per sentence."** NPR itself introduces it with "It's often said". Working-memory evidence supports fewer clauses and canonical order (rules 17, 19); no study tests "one idea" as a unit.
- **"Write it, read it aloud, cut a third."** The read-aloud step is universally documented; the "cut a third" quantity traces to no author. NPR's example cuts nothing, it splits (rule 29).
- **"Videos must be under 6 minutes."** Guo et al.'s median-engagement ceiling is from 2014 edX lecture chunks; the transferable part is that engagement decays with length and chunking helped.
- **Radiolab "does not work from written scripts"** versus Ira Glass's account of a hybrid script read word for word for technical passages: both true of different projects. The transferable point is that explanatory passages are scripted exactly and connective chat is ad-libbed and re-recorded after listening.
- **Disney's twelve principles aid comprehension.** Well-attested craft, and Heer & Robertson give a perceptual rationale for slow-in/slow-out (predictability), but no controlled study of these principles' effect on understanding in explainers was found.
